<p class="lede">Nobody sent a memo. There was no morning where SwiftUI stopped being the interesting-but-risky option and became the obvious one. It happened the way infrastructure shifts always happen: gradually, and then in retrospect.</p>

<p>If you are starting an iOS app today and reaching for UIKit out of caution, the caution is now the risk.</p>

<h2>What actually changed</h2>

<p>The early argument against SwiftUI was never about the idea. Declarative interfaces had already won on the web. The argument was about <em>readiness</em> — missing controls, unpredictable layout, a list that could not do the one thing your designer wanted.</p>

<p>Three things quietly fixed that:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>The gaps closed.</strong> The controls people used to drop to UIKit for are there now, and they behave.</li>
  <li><strong>Apple's own apps moved.</strong> That is the signal that matters. Frameworks Apple ships against are frameworks Apple fixes.</li>
  <li><strong>Interop stopped being painful.</strong> Wrapping a UIKit view is a few lines, so "SwiftUI cannot do X" became "SwiftUI hosts X" — a footnote, not a decision.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Why it makes better apps, not just faster ones</h2>

<p>The productivity story gets all the attention — less code, live previews, no more hunting a constraint conflict at 11pm. True, and not the interesting part.</p>

<blockquote>In UIKit, state and screen are two things you keep in sync. In SwiftUI, the screen is a function of the state. Whole categories of bug simply stop existing.</blockquote>

<p>The bugs that vanish are the ones users actually report: the toggle that shows the wrong value after you background the app, the row that keeps a stale label after a refresh, the button still spinning after the request finished. Those are all synchronisation bugs. Remove the synchronisation and you remove the bugs.</p>

<p>You also get the things teams usually promise and postpone, largely for free:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Dynamic Type</strong> that genuinely reflows instead of clipping.</li>
  <li><strong>Dark mode</strong> as a consequence of using semantic colours, not a second design pass.</li>
  <li><strong>VoiceOver</strong> that mostly works before anyone has audited it, because the structure is declared rather than assembled.</li>
</ul>

<p>Accessibility stops being a line item you cut and becomes something you would have to work to break.</p>

<h2>What it still cannot do</h2>

<p>Anyone selling you SwiftUI without this section is selling you something:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Very large collections</strong> with heavy per-cell work still reward UIKit's precise control over reuse.</li>
  <li><strong>Deep text editing</strong> — a real rich-text editor — still means TextKit underneath.</li>
  <li><strong>Bleeding-edge layout</strong> occasionally needs a UIKit escape hatch, and that is fine. Reaching for one is not a failure.</li>
  <li><strong>Older OS support.</strong> If you must support several versions back, some of the nicest APIs are not available to you yet.</li>
</ul>

<h2>So what should you do</h2>

<p><strong>New app?</strong> SwiftUI, with UIKit available where you need it. You will ship sooner and support it more cheaply.</p>

<p><strong>Existing UIKit app?</strong> Do not rewrite it. Nobody thanks you for a rewrite. Build the next screen in SwiftUI, host it in what you have, and let the codebase migrate one feature at a time.</p>

<p>The future arrived quietly. It is fine to arrive quietly too.</p>
