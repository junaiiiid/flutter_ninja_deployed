<p class="lede">Ask most companies about their AI feature and you will be shown a chat box. Type a question, get a paragraph. It is genuinely useful and it is also the least interesting thing a model can do for you.</p>

<p>The gap between that chat box and something that actually removes work is not a better model. It is three specific capabilities, and they have to be built in order.</p>

<h2>Tier 1 — Grounding</h2>

<p>A general model knows an enormous amount about the world and nothing whatsoever about your company. Grounding fixes that: the model answers from <em>your</em> documents, contracts, tickets and policies, and cites where each answer came from. The industry calls this retrieval-augmented generation — RAG.</p>

<p>The part people underestimate is the refusal. A grounded system that says <strong>"I don't know"</strong> is worth more than one that always produces something, because the first can be trusted and the second has to be checked. And a tool that has to be checked has not saved anybody any time.</p>

<blockquote>The hard part of RAG is not the model. It is retrieval and evaluation — getting the right five paragraphs in front of it, and being able to prove how often you did.</blockquote>

<p>Tier 1 alone usually pays for itself on support load. Many teams should stop here for six months.</p>

<h2>Tier 2 — Memory</h2>

<p>Now the agent knows your content. It still does not know <em>who it is talking to</em>.</p>

<p>Memory means the agent has the user's history, preferences, plan and position in your product. The effect is subtle and enormous: it stops asking what it should already know. No more "which account is this about?" when the person is signed in and looking at the account.</p>

<ul>
  <li>Answers become specific to a person instead of generically correct.</li>
  <li>Conversations resume instead of restarting.</li>
  <li>The system can be proactive — the trial is ending, the invoice failed, the export is ready.</li>
</ul>

<p>This is also the tier where privacy stops being a policy document and becomes an architecture decision. Storing what a person told you is a promise about how it will be used.</p>

<h2>Tier 3 — Actions</h2>

<p>Here the assistant becomes a colleague. The agent <em>does</em> things: fills the form, updates the setting, creates the record, moves the job through the workflow. This is tool use, and it is where the value stops being conversational and starts being operational.</p>

<p>It is also where an unserious implementation becomes a liability. The guardrails are not optional extras:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Scoped permissions.</strong> An agent must never be able to do more than the signed-in user could do themselves.</li>
  <li><strong>Confirmation before anything irreversible.</strong> No silent deletions. No surprise payments.</li>
  <li><strong>A full audit trail</strong> of what it did and why — the thing that makes this defensible to your customers and your lawyers.</li>
  <li><strong>Honest failure.</strong> "I could not do that" beats a confident report of work that never happened.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Why the order is not negotiable</h2>

<p>Every tier makes the next one safer. Grounding gives the agent facts, so memory has something true to personalise. Memory gives it context, so actions are taken on the right record.</p>

<p>Skip to actions and you have built something that acts confidently on a guess. That is not an AI feature; that is an incident waiting for a date. The teams that got burned in the last two years almost all skipped a tier.</p>

<h2>Where to start</h2>

<p>Pick the workflow you would most like to hand over — the one your team does twenty times a week and nobody enjoys. Then ask two questions: <em>what would the agent need to know to do this?</em> and <em>how would I find out if it did it wrong?</em></p>

<p>If you can answer both, you have a tier-one project with a clear path to tier three. If you cannot answer the second, you have found the actual work.</p>
